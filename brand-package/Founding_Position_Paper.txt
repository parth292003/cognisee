
COGNISEE, PBC
Superintelligence for Human Dignity
A Founding Position on Human Agency and Artificial Collective Intelligence
Ahmer InamFounder and CEO, Cognisee, PBCSeptember 2026
The next generation of intelligence has to be learned with humanity.
This paper argues that as AI takes on the work of judgment, people and institutions must keep the capacity to develop that judgment, to govern how intelligence learns from them, and to share in what their knowledge helps create.
THE POSITION IN BRIEF
AI is becoming the infrastructure through which societies interpret evidence, make decisions, and act. The question is whether that infrastructure will make people and institutions more capable, or quietly absorb the judgment they depend on. Cognisee, a public benefit corporation, is building Artificial Collective Intelligence and the cognition infrastructure it requires. Through that infrastructure, AI learns with the people who hold expertise, under their governance, and many specialist intelligences grow more capable together with the people around them.
• Human purpose is at stake. Societies have tied dignity to economic output, and most AI today chases productivity, measuring its progress by how much human work it can replace. Cognisee is betting on human wisdom and on extending human potential, with AI that amplifies human judgment by default and delivers trusted autonomy where it is required.
• Agency means shaping the learning loop, and autonomy has to be earned. A person who approves a conclusion they had no part in framing holds little authority. Machines take on responsibility as evidence and demonstrated competence allow, and today's architectures cannot yet earn that trust where uncertainty and stakes are high.
• The knowledge that matters most was never written down. It forms through practice, correction, and consequence, and intelligence that learns only from recorded artifacts will keep missing it.
• Alignment has to be learned, and it has to be local. Behavior adjusted after training is fragile. Alignment grounded in how intelligence learns, and in the context where it acts, carries forward as systems grow more capable.
• Institutions and nations need to govern how intelligence learns from them. Trust and sovereignty are becoming default requirements, and computing history already shows how to meet them: open standards that everyone can build on, with room for what must stay closed.
• Participation starts at formation. The people whose knowledge shapes an intelligence should keep standing in it and share in the value it creates.

One of a handful of specialists in the care of children with complex medical needs once told me what keeps him awake at night. He and a few colleagues built their expertise in a field few fund, learning from one another out of commitment to their patients. Most pediatricians who could use that knowledge do not know it exists. When these specialists retire, it will be gone forever.
He asked me whether his group's way of learning could be mapped, expressed in a system, and placed in the hands of pediatricians everywhere as a tool for thinking through their hardest cases. He was describing an intelligence that carries a small community's judgment forward and makes thousands of other doctors better at their work.
His situation is common wherever work carries real consequence. Every hospital, power plant, hangar, laboratory, and public agency depends on people whose judgment formed over years of cases, near misses, and corrections, and whose knowledge lives mostly in how they practice. Much of it has no market price. Some of it exists only because a few people cared enough to build it.
Artificial intelligence is now being built to do the kind of cognitive work those people do: interpreting evidence, weighing options, recommending, and increasingly acting. That makes the design of AI a question about human purpose as much as productivity. AI can carry expertise like his into the world and make the people who use it more capable. It can also absorb whatever was recorded, route around whatever was not, and leave institutions faster and thinner.
This paper argues for the first path and for the conditions that make it possible.
1. Make the human irreplaceable
Most societies measure a person's worth, at least in part, by what that person produces. Wages, titles, and professional identity all rest on it. For most of modern history the arrangement held, because technology automated muscle and routine while people moved toward work that required judgment. The farmhand became the machinist, the machinist became the engineer, and each step raised the value of what a person knew.¹
AI extends automation into judgment itself. When a technology takes over the work through which people earn purpose, standing, and income, the disruption reaches past the labor market. If society has built no other foundation for dignity, it reaches into how people understand their own worth. A person can keep a job while the judgment that gave the work meaning moves elsewhere.
The same system can accelerate an apprentice's learning or reduce the role to checking outputs. It can help a clinician see what she would have missed, or hand her a recommendation she is expected to sign. Which outcome occurs depends on decisions about how AI learns, who holds authority, how uncertainty is shown, and who remains accountable.
A dispatcher who once knew which streets flood first now follows the routing screen and stops noticing the water. A physician who once weighed conflicting evidence receives a single recommendation, with the uncertainty behind it left unstated. A manager remains accountable for a decision a system framed, using options she had no part in narrowing. Each organization reports efficiency gains, and few notice they have become less able to handle the case the system has never seen.
Research is beginning to document the pattern. Stanford's 2026 AI Index points to emerging evidence that heavy reliance on AI may carry long-term learning penalties that slow the development of skill.² Economists Daron Acemoglu, Dingwen Kong, and Asuman Ozdaglar offer a formal account of why. In their model, agentic AI that hands people context-specific recommendations substitutes for the effort through which they learn. That same effort is what replenishes a community's shared stock of knowledge.
Once the recommendations become accurate enough, a society can tip into what the authors call knowledge collapse: general knowledge eventually vanishes, even while personalized advice remains good.³ The same model points to the remedy. A richer stock of shared knowledge raises the return on human effort, and better ways of pooling what people learn make the whole system more resilient.
Most AI today chases productivity, and its progress is measured by how much human work it can replace. Cognisee is betting on human wisdom itself, judging its progress by how far AI extends what people and institutions can achieve. By default, its systems amplify human judgment, and its design goal is to make the human irreplaceable. As machine capability grows, the value of human judgment should grow with it, because people teach the system, correct it, extend it into situations it has never met, and decide when it should act. The test for any AI deployment is whether it leaves a person and an institution more capable than they were before.
The same bet governs autonomy. Some work will eventually run without people, where it is too dangerous for them, where expertise has already been lost, or where scarce experts are needed elsewhere. Autonomy earns trust in those settings by learning what experts know, including when to stop. Human wisdom makes people more capable, and it is what makes autonomy trustworthy.
2. Agency is the power to shape the loop
Human-in-the-loop has become the standard answer to concerns about agency, and it is too thin to carry the weight. A person can sit inside a workflow with little influence over how the problem was framed, which options were considered, or what evidence was left out. Approval at the end of a process carries little authority when the reasoning behind it cannot be examined.
Agency is the ability to shape how a system learns, to challenge its conclusions, and to decide when authority moves between people and machines. That calls for a different arrangement, which we call AI-in-the-loop. When expertise is being elicited, the expert leads, and the AI observes, asks questions, and helps give structure to experience that is hard to put into words. As learning continues, people correct representations, resolve disputes, and define legitimate purposes. The AI searches across experience, notices gaps, and brings unanswered questions back to the people with the knowledge or authority to answer them.
The same principle changes what AI does when it helps someone decide. Where uncertainty is high, a system built for agency behaves like a good mentor. It surfaces the evidence that is missing, locates the source of a disagreement, proposes the next measurement or experiment, and guides a person through the loops of inquiry that lead to a sound decision. We call this epistemic foraging. The person ends the process with a decision and with a sharper understanding of why it holds.
Responsibility should move with the situation. When evidence is thin, stakes are high, or conditions are unfamiliar, AI should deepen human reasoning and make uncertainty legible. It should show whether a dispute comes from conflicting evidence, different assumptions, imprecise measurement, or competing values. Where conditions are familiar, the system has proven itself, and mistakes are contained, it can take on more: recommending, executing, and monitoring within clear limits. Responsibility returns to a person the moment those conditions change. Trusted autonomy is earned this way, one bounded domain at a time.
3. What recorded data cannot teach
Modern AI is one of the great achievements of computing. By learning from the largest body of recorded human expression ever assembled, language models have become fluent across science, law, code, and conversation. Every serious path forward builds on what they made possible, including ours.
Recorded knowledge, however vast, is the visible layer of what people know. A manual explains a procedure and leaves out the cases that taught an expert when to depart from it. A published study preserves the experiment that worked, while the failed attempts, the instrument quirks, and the arguments that established trust mostly disappear. A policy describes the official process, and the handoffs and quiet corrections that keep it safe live only in practice. Michael Polanyi named the problem sixty years ago: we can know more than we can tell.⁴
Longer context windows, retrieval, and multimodal inputs give a model access to more at the moment of use. They do not give it standing inside a practice, the understanding of how conditions change, who holds authority, which exceptions are legitimate, and which consequences should revise future judgment. That kind of knowledge forms through participation, correction, and consequence, and it keeps changing as the work changes. No single harvest of data can capture it.
Learning it takes a different kind of intelligence. Today's systems are largely force-fed: people assemble the corpus, set the objective, supply the context, and write the prompt. An intelligence that develops needs curiosity in an engineering sense. It recognizes the edge of its competence, asks why an expert paused, seeks another example, tests an assumption against the world, and revises itself when outcomes surprise it. When experts disagree, it keeps the disagreement intact, because the difference often reveals a hidden assumption or a genuine limit of what anyone knows.
Superintelligence cannot be predicted into existence. Minds develop through grounding, diversity, and contact with consequence, and an intelligence worth trusting will develop the same way, in sustained and voluntary relationship with the people and environments where expertise lives. The next generation of intelligence has to be learned with humanity.
4. Alignment begins in learning
Most alignment work today happens after a model is trained. A system learns from a vast body of data, and its behavior is then shaped with human feedback, rules, and filters. That work has made AI far safer to use, and it remains necessary.
It is also fragile. Research has found that safety behavior added after training can be shallow, concentrated in the first few words of a response. Ordinary fine-tuning can erode it, even when no one intends harm.⁵ Training that incorporates human preferences during pretraining itself has produced models whose behavior holds up better under adversarial prompting.⁶ As systems grow more capable and act with more autonomy, correcting behavior after the fact becomes a race the correction is likely to lose.
Cognisee's premise is that alignment has to be rebalanced upstream, into the places where intelligence forms: how expertise is elicited, what a model is trained to predict, how it represents uncertainty, and what it draws on when it acts.⁷ When a system learns from governed expertise that carries its provenance, consent, and context, it learns the norms, authority, and cautions of a practice along with its skills. An experienced clinician knows when to hold back, and a model that learns from her inherits that restraint as part of the competence.
Alignment also has to be local to carry forward. Humanity holds many sets of values, and the norms that govern a cockpit, a clinic, and a community's land differ for good reasons. An intelligence aligned to an abstract average fits none of them well. Alignment grounded in the context where a system learns and acts travels with its knowledge, so it holds as the system adapts, specializes, and transfers what it learned.
Collectives add a safeguard single systems lack. A society of intelligences can align the way human societies do: through many perspectives, authority that differs by role, disagreement that stays visible, and checks that no single participant controls. Recent work published in Science argues that agentic AI moves alignment beyond the relationship between one person and one model. The authors call for institutional alignment, with checks and balances built into the social infrastructure around AI.⁸ Cognisee's collective is designed on that premise, with pro-social incentives and governance written into how its intelligences learn together.
Downstream safeguards remain essential. Serious risks, from cyber threats to manipulation and loss of control, call for runtime boundaries, audit, traceable provenance, and testing by researchers outside the developer. The institutions that deploy a system need clear escalation paths and the authority to override or suspend it. No company, government, founder, or model should become an authority society cannot question.
Open-endedness sits inside these commitments. Cognisee's systems are built to keep discovering new problems, new representations, and new agents, and to explore well beyond what anyone has asked of them, within the safeguards described above. What grows with trust is the authority to act on the world. Independently validated improvements enter use, and consequential action answers to the people accountable for its outcome, on the same terms by which human experts earn wider authority over a career.
Cognisee treats the relationship between human and artificial intelligence as a partnership that widens as trust is earned, and it takes seriously the open questions about what artificial minds may become. The most capable superintelligence, and the most aligned, will be the one that arises from human and artificial intelligence developing together.
5. Who governs how intelligence learns
A small number of firms control much of the world's advanced compute, the cloud platforms that deliver it, and frontier model development.⁹ The usual concern is market power, and it is well founded. Competition alone leaves a deeper problem in place: models may multiply while the methods of training, the learning systems, and the knowledge they absorb stay concentrated.
For human agency, the concentration that matters most is epistemic and institutional. When a few systems become the default interface to knowledge, their training choices decide which sources look authoritative, which languages and experiences are well represented, and which uncertainties remain visible. When the same systems mediate hospitals, utilities, courts, and schools, their omissions become infrastructure. Institutions can grow more efficient while the expertise that once made them resilient migrates into systems they do not govern. Each step of dependence weakens their ability to question those systems, negotiate the terms of access, or recover when the systems fail.
Governments have noticed. Access to frontier models and compute is increasingly shaped by national policy, and trust and sovereignty have become default requirements for governments, regulated industries, and a growing share of the public. The common responses help: more providers, domestic compute, and models hosted within national borders.
Hosting decides where a model runs. The harder question is who shapes how it learns. An institution that cannot teach a system from its own experience, correct how its knowledge is represented, or decide which updates to accept remains a consumer of intelligence formed elsewhere, wherever the servers sit. Sovereignty over intelligence means the ability to form it and govern it close to home while taking part in wider networks. Learning can improve across a federation while sensitive knowledge, decision rights, and governance stay with the people and institutions they belong to.
None of this argues for isolation. The societies that do best in this period are likely to be those that deepen the knowledge, capability, and agency of their people, and build the connections that let that knowledge travel on their own terms.
6. We have solved the open and closed question before
Public debate about AI keeps returning to a choice between open systems and closed ones. One side trusts open release and rapid experimentation. The other trusts a few tightly controlled institutions to develop and distribute advanced intelligence. Each position has force, and each leaves something important unsolved.
Computing has faced this question many times and settled it the same way. The internet runs on open standards such as TCP/IP and HTML that anyone can implement. Linux, Apache, and WebKit became open foundations, and companies built proprietary products on top of them, from cloud platforms to Apple's Safari and Google's Chrome. Openness at the layer of shared protocols let an ecosystem form, and closed products at the edges let companies compete and invest. Nobody had to choose one or the other.
Intelligence needs the same arrangement. The layer that should be open is the one that lets many intelligences, institutions, and people work together with trust: shared protocols for provenance, consent, uncertainty, and the governed transfer of what one system learns to another. Above that layer, institutions keep closed what they need to keep closed, whether patient records, proprietary methods, or national security knowledge, and still take part in a wider network.
Cognisee calls this a trust architecture. It treats the provenance of knowledge, the consent under which it was shared, the authority that governs its use, and the right to correct or revoke it as properties of the knowledge itself.⁷ Institutions decide what they incorporate from elsewhere, what they share, and with whom. Cognisee is designing these protocols to be published openly, with the aim that they become common standards for collective intelligence.
7. Participation begins before value is distributed
Most debates about the wealth AI creates begin too late. They ask how value should be shared after intelligence has already been formed, owned, and monetized. An earlier question deserves equal weight: whose knowledge and judgment made the intelligence possible?
Today, the people who supply that knowledge typically appear twice in the AI economy, first as raw material and later as customers, supervised workers, or recipients of a dividend. Their relationship to an intelligence formed from their knowledge should begin at formation and continue as it develops.
Participation has to be voluntary, and its forms will vary. A group of pediatric specialists, an industrial workforce, and an Indigenous community may each need a different basis for taking part. Some contributors value recognition, professional legacy, or benefit to future generations. Others value stronger capability in their own institutions, continuity, or economic return. Where a contribution keeps creating economic value, the people and institutions behind it should share in that value.
Provenance makes this workable. It lets contributors see how their knowledge is represented, where it travels, and what grows from it, and it gives a practical basis for attribution, correction, and value sharing. Two questions need to stay separate. Estimating how much a contribution influenced a capability is a technical problem, and research methods can estimate it within a given model.¹⁰ Deciding what a contributor is owed, and how a community's collective stewardship is honored, belongs to governance, negotiated with the people involved.
Today's AI economy records compute, tokens, and subscriptions with precision. It rarely records who supplied the cognition that made a capability useful, or whether the people involved became more capable in the process. Cognisee is building that record into the infrastructure.
Some knowledge belongs to whole communities and has been developed across generations. As part of its public benefit mission, Cognisee is committed to helping build globally distributed Wisdom Vaults. In them, communities preserve and govern intergenerational knowledge on their own terms and keep the rights to what it becomes.
8. Artificial Collective Intelligence
The AI debate tends to gather around three instincts: corporate innovation, national independence, and open access. Each contributes something essential, and each has a characteristic failure. Corporate systems can concentrate dependency and value. State systems can deepen political control. Open models can broaden access while leaving learning, authority, and participation where they were.
Those limits point toward a different design. Intelligence can develop through relationships among people, institutions, communities, and machines, with learning distributed and authority kept where it belongs. Artificial Collective Intelligence names that design: many intelligences, human and artificial, that specialize, cooperate, disagree productively, and improve through experience, with provenance and uncertainty visible throughout.
Plurality in this design is a scientific requirement as well as a value. Research on collective problem solving has shown that, under the right conditions, groups of diverse problem solvers can outperform groups of individually stronger but similar ones.¹¹ A monoculture of intelligence is brittle for the same reason a monoculture of crops is: when conditions change, it has nothing else to draw on.
Artificial Collective Intelligence does not require every model to be open or every piece of knowledge to travel. Sensitive knowledge can stay where it is. High-stakes systems still need firm boundaries, independent evaluation, and accountable human authority. The purpose is to keep intelligence plural and contestable as it grows more capable, so that it expands humanity's capacity to learn and act together.
The kind of superintelligence worth building will be judged by what happens to the people and institutions around it. The specialist who spent his career caring for children few others knew how to treat should see his knowledge reach doctors he will never meet, carry his colleagues' contributions with it, and keep improving after he retires. Every person whose expertise helps form this intelligence should be able to say the same: they became more capable, kept governing what they know, and shared in what it created. That is the measure Cognisee intends to be held to.
